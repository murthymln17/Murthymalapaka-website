Intelligence Has to Earn the Name

4 min read

Intelligence implies purpose — solving a problem humans can’t solve in time, at consistent quality, or at scale. From business intelligence to AI coding, we keep attaching the word to tools that never stated the problem.

I have never understood why a whole category of software was called business intelligence.

Most of what carried that label was a query tool with good charts. You asked a question, it returned an answer. It never knew why you asked. It didn’t know which outcome the business was trying to move, whether the number on the dashboard was good or bad, or what should happen next. A human did all of that. The intelligence was in the analyst, not the tool.

That isn’t a knock on what those tools did. Giving people access to data mattered. But the name promised something the tools never delivered, and I think we’re repeating that mistake with AI.

What the word should mean

Intelligence implies purpose. It’s in the word. Being intelligent means solving a problem intelligently: reaching a better outcome than you would have otherwise.

For enterprise systems, I’d say intelligence earns the name when it does three things:

  • It serves a stated purpose. There is a business outcome it is accountable for, such as claims cycle time, order-to-delivery or onboarding speed, and it is measured against that outcome, not against its own activity.
  • It does what humans can’t do well enough. That means work a person can’t do in the time available, can’t do with consistent quality, or can’t do at the scale the business needs.
  • It leads to a decision or an action. It doesn’t just return what was asked. It reaches a disposition and moves the outcome.

Take any one of these away and you have a faster tool, not an intelligent one.

The same trap, new clothes

Communications of the ACM published a piece this week on scaling AI-enabled coding. It cites CircleCI’s 2026 State of Software Delivery: more code was being written, yet the rate of finished, released work fell. AI-generated code failed more often and took longer to fix.

One line in the piece stands out: “Writing code was never really the bottleneck.”

Run AI coding through the test above. Does it serve a stated purpose? Mostly it has been measured on code generated and developer velocity, which describe activity, not outcomes. Does it do what humans can’t? It types faster, but typing was never the constraint. Does it move the outcome? The delivery data says not yet.

This is business intelligence all over again. A capability arrived, the word “intelligent” got attached, and the problem was never stated. AI coding came in the way full self-driving did: exciting and new, but not something anyone was asking for. An electric vehicle answered a plain need, which was lower fuel cost. Technology push isn’t wrong in itself, but at some point the need has to be named.

The article also introduces Margaret-Anne Storey’s idea of intent debt: the missing record of goals, constraints and rationale, so nobody knows what a system is actually for. That’s the cost of intelligence without purpose, and it doesn’t start in the codebase. It starts when nobody writes down which business outcome the work is meant to serve.

How we started: purpose first

When I was asked to build a platform for managed services that could be positioned and sold differently, the brief didn’t mention a model. It came down to a question: what would make this different?

The answer was intelligence built from historical operational data. As we worked through years of that data, a pattern stood out: operations quietly failing a promise. Over time I came to name that promise peak business performance. That became the purpose, and elimination became the way to deliver it. Eliminate the work that shouldn’t exist, automate what can’t be eliminated, and bring in human assist where judgment is needed.

Then came the “humans can’t do this well enough” test. Many steps in our data pipeline were extremely difficult or slow for engineers to do at scale, and two engineers looking at the same history would reach different conclusions. That’s what justified the models. We started with classic AI and moved to generative AI as the work required it.

The models were chosen because a stated purpose needed them. It didn’t work the other way around.

The question to ask before calling anything intelligent

Before an enterprise calls its next investment “intelligent,” whether it’s an agent, a copilot, an AI coding platform or a dashboard, it should be able to answer three questions in one sentence each:

  • 1. Which business outcome is this accountable for?
  • 2. What can it do that our people can’t do in time, consistently, or at scale?
  • 3. What decision or action does it take, and what did it eliminate?

If those answers are missing, it may still be a useful tool. It just hasn’t earned the name.
