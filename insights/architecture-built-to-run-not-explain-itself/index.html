Your Architecture Was Built to Run, Not to Explain Itself

6 min read

Intelligence can find the anomaly and explain it. Only a reworked architecture can act on it.

I was reading a HackerNoon piece on monitoring event-driven architectures this week, and one line stayed with me: successfully publishing an event does not mean the business process completed successfully.

That sentence describes far more than event-driven systems. It describes how most enterprise architecture has been designed for decades. We built systems to run. We never built them to explain themselves.

The broker is healthy. The business is not.

The article lays out a familiar problem. In a point-to-point design, failures surface on their own through connection errors and missing acknowledgments. In an event-driven design, the broker queues messages when a consumer disconnects. Nothing is lost, so the broker looks healthy, even when processing has quietly stopped.

The author gives two failure modes worth thinking about. A stop-sell event fans out to regional channel servers, and not all of them receive or process it. A consumer instance crashes but keeps receiving messages without acknowledging them, so messages pile up and compute is wasted.

The recommended answer is a sensible combination: APM tools evaluating multiple conditions together, application-level publish and consume rates, and heartbeat events that validate the path end to end.

All of it is good engineering. And all of it is static. Alert when a critical queue has more than five spooled messages and no bound client, three times in ten minutes. Investigate when throughput drops, and accept the false positives. These are human guesses encoded as thresholds.

The real gap is not detection. It is that none of these signals can tell you whether a business outcome is at risk, why, or what to do about it.

Numbers tell you what. Text tells you why.

Most of my work before generative AI was on metric streams and time series. Incident avoidance, which I started building about ten years ago, began by extracting business KPIs from historical data, setting up time-series observation on them, and then layering infrastructure metric streams underneath. Anomaly detection on numbers is powerful. It tells you something changed and when.

What it cannot tell you is what that change means.

That is where our journey moved from classic AI to generative AI. We used GenAI to read through textual data, including logs, tickets and user complaints, and build dispositions for decisions and causation.

  • A user complaint confirms the anomaly. When a user says order processing is slow, and a synthetic transaction monitor shows order processing time climbing at the same moment, you have two independent signals pointing at the same business impact. The text reinforces the numbers.
  • Text points toward causation. Logs and ticket history carry the context that metrics strip away: which step, which dependency, which change, which pattern has been seen before.
  • Together, they earn the right to act. Correlation alone should never trigger an automated action on infrastructure or applications. Correlation reinforced by text, and validated against historical patterns, can.

The simplest way I can put it: classic AI finds the anomaly, GenAI explains it, and the architecture has to be able to act on it.

That last part is where most enterprises are stuck.

Bolting intelligence onto mute systems

Whether the architecture is event-driven, request-response, batch or a mainframe at the core, the pattern is the same. Every one of these can be reinforced with intelligence today. But most were never designed to take part in it.

Logs were written for a developer debugging at 2 a.m., not for correlation with business entities. Telemetry describes components, not outcomes. And there is rarely a safe, governed way for an intelligent system to do anything other than raise an alert.

So enterprises bolt AI on top. They add an AIOps layer, an observability platform, an agent. The intelligence can see more, but it is still working with systems that cannot explain themselves and cannot accept corrective action. That caps the value, no matter how good the model is.

This is the same mistake we make with process. Automating a broken process just gives you a faster broken process. That is why I argue for eliminating first, then automating, then seeking human assist. Architecture deserves the same treatment. Before you layer intelligence on top, rework the architecture so it can take part in its own intelligence.

What the rework looks like

This is not a rip-and-replace argument. It is a design argument. Three changes matter most.

Emit meaning, not just telemetry. Systems should carry business context in what they emit: the order, the claim, the customer, the process step. Events should carry their expected outcome and the time within which it should happen. Consumers should confirm that the business outcome happened, not just that a message arrived. That alone closes the gap the HackerNoon piece describes: the stop-sell event was published, but did every region actually stop selling?

Expose safe ways to act. Intelligence that cannot act stops at a notification to email or Teams. Applications and infrastructure need governed action points: scale, divert, restart, replay, roll back. In my own work, safe diversion became a core action, meaning an alternate path known to take the load, the way full self-driving moves over instead of braking hard. The objective is near-zero MTTR with no business disruption, not a faster page to an on-call engineer. That requires the diversion paths, idempotent replay and limits on how much a single action can affect to be designed in, not discovered during an incident.

Close the feedback loop. Every action and its outcome should flow back into historical intelligence. What was detected, what the text said, what action was taken, and whether the business outcome recovered. This is how the system learns what fixed what, and how humans stay in the loop to correct it, until the track record earns more autonomy.

APM doesn’t go away. Its job changes.

None of this makes APM tools, broker statistics or heartbeats obsolete. They cover real gaps, and they should stay.

What changes is their role. Today they mostly end in dashboards and alerts for a human to interpret. In an intelligence-ready architecture, they become inputs: numeric signals fused with textual signals, evaluated against historical patterns of good and bad behavior, feeding decisions that the architecture is built to carry out.

The foundation under all of it is the one I keep coming back to: discovery and dependency mapping attached to past behaviors. Loose coupling is good design, but it hides who depends on whom. The more loosely coupled the architecture, the more that map matters, because the dependencies are no longer visible in the code.

The question for architects

Every architecture review I have seen asks whether the system will scale, whether it is secure, and whether it can be monitored.

I would add one more question: can this system explain itself, and can it accept corrective action?

If the answer is no, the intelligence you buy will see the problem, describe it beautifully, and then wait for a human, just like the threshold alert it replaced.

Architectures that have run enterprises for decades are due for this kind of renewal. Not because the technology is legacy, but because they were built to run, and the next decade will ask them to explain themselves and act.
